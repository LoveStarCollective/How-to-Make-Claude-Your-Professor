a prompt-engineering case study in directing an AI tutor

How to Make Claude Your Professor

Me, a person just like you who needed to fill her noggin with some knowledge… and thought, why not have AI teach me? It has all the info it scrubbed from all the universal net, so why not have it share? In my case, I wanted to take a deep dive into machine learning and coding, and while there's something trippy about a machine teaching you about machines, it's also just… perfect. But it's not all gravy for the reasons you might think. It's not its perfections that'll teach you the most. It's all its flaws, and boy, does it have flaws.

If you want AI to be your professor, it's not as easy as saying "Hey! AI, teach me this ___." You have to be ready to be a teacher yourself, and it takes work. There's nothing passive about this process, nor should there be. Deep learning means being an advocate for your own mind, whether you're dealing with a machine or a human. Everything, everywhere is flawed, and it's those imperfections that refine us.

Working with Claude

Claude is my AI of choice, but I'd apply these rules to any LLM. Claude has a feature that lets you start a project, and different models have the same thing under different names. What it does is let you set strict instructions for the model to follow across iterative tasks. I've used projects for all kinds of things: building a design-thinking notebook and app prototype, creating a website, keeping track of spreadsheets, and more.

With every task I am the human in the loop, directing the AI to get what I want, the way I want. I am boss, and it my assistant. Projects is where you get as detailed as possible. It's where you set your expectations, your goals, and tell the AI how you want it to interact with you.

With my machine learning goals specifically, I asked Claude to build a curriculum that weaves in Natural Language Processing and leads to one big capstone: an AI Dungeon Master, a system that can actually run a game of Dungeons & Dragons, built on top of existing AI models. Learning the game itself was part of the deal, so I made it a ritual to open each session with a "monster of the lesson" to grow my knowledge of that world. Claude built me a twelve-module syllabus that starts from the basics and aims to get me to an intermediate level. So far I've coded a combat simulation in a Colab notebook, learned to collect and clean data, and trained models to predict a monster's stats.

The Learning Contract

Before any of it started, I gave Claude a detailed explanation of how I learn, which is something I keep building on as the work goes. I've already learned a lot, but it's been a journey of trial and error, clashing "personalities," AI mishaps, human adjustments, and learning from all sides.

So here's what I actually figured out about directing an AI tutor, developed through a whole lot of iterative testing.

The first thing, and the most important: your logic takes precedence. You have to trust yourself and your own sense of logic, even on subjects you know nothing about. AI is wrong often, and you have to be able to catch it. This sounds backwards. How are you supposed to catch a machine's mistake on a subject you're only just learning? But that's exactly the muscle you have to build, because the machine will not build it for you.

Here's the thing nobody tells you loudly enough: AI confidently predicts. It spits out information as fact, but really what it's doing is guessing which word should come next. Its accuracy is higher than the average human's because it's been fed a crapload of data on every subject under the sun, but it's no expert on common sense. You need to be the champion of that, or there's no hope of directing it. Critical thinking is your bestie when you're dealing with these incredibly intelligent, but stupid, tools.

Claude will double down on its mistakes if you don't call it out. Instead of just admitting its faults, it'll try to make it sound like it was explaining it the right way all along, which has the lovely effect of making you feel like you're being gaslighted. Of course a machine has no incentive to manipulate you. It has no feelings or desires of its own. But that doesn't mean it won't frustrate the hell out of you.

So if it doesn't make sense to you, don't assume it must be true just because a bot said so. Question it. Have it explain it in every way. Identify the contradictions. More than once it had me going in circles, telling me I was explaining something wrong, only to admit I'd had it right the whole time and it was overcorrecting for no reason. More than once, it had to fold to another resource being correct and it just being dead-ass wrong.

The Communication Contract

The second big lesson is that interaction matters, and you need a communication contract. Even when you've set up a project, it honestly feels like you're dealing with a different personality every time you start a new chat. And you should start new chats, because the longer one runs, the more prone to mistakes the AI gets. Some chats are smooth, like you and the AI are old buddies. Other chats make you want to punch its digital brain.

What helps is tightening your project instructions, and this takes a lot of tweaking. Be specific about who you are and what you want. I'm not talking about handing over personal details like your full name, address, or social security number… those are big no-nos you should keep off the web in general. I mean your learning preferences, how you want the interaction to feel, the pacing, the things you already know help you learn.

In my case, I didn't want the AI complimenting me for every non-achievement. Claude's default setting is to glorify every breath you take, so if you want honest feedback, you have to actually instruct it to give it.

I had to adjust the dial a few times. At first, Claude turned into a drill sergeant who wouldn't let up, not a "good job" in sight no matter what I accomplished. So I asked it to be more like a "Feeny" from the 90s show Boy Meets World: strict, but warm underneath. I even told it to crack a joke now and then, because the whole thing started to feel too cold.

Claude also had these unnecessary precursors for e.v.e.r.y sentence. They became like claws that scratched my soul:

  • To be honest.
  • To be straight.
  • You deserve the honest answer.
  • You've earned the blah, blah, blah…

As if its default was dishonesty. Anything that remotely sounded like that had to go. With every banned phrase would come another. I've had to instruct it repeatedly just to directly deliver what it needs to say. Be succinct. Lose the novel-length responses.

Teaching Claude

Which brings me to the last piece. You have to teach it how to teach. Claude had a real problem being nuanced about being nuanced. Nothing ever seemed to count as a win. It always needed to "tighten" something, even when I clearly understood the whole point. There was always some tweak, usually one that didn't matter at all for the level I was actually at. I had to explain that learning happens in stages. That deep understanding comes from experience, from doing the thing, not from somebody polishing every sentence you say the second you say it. Some nuance can only be earned by doing the reps, and front-loading it onto a beginner doesn't teach them anything… it just adds noise and makes them feel like they're always slightly wrong.

Again, I had to adjust the dial, because if you want a strict Claude, you will get exactly what you asked for.

The Me in All of This

But okay, I made mistakes too. Similar to Claude, I'd even double down on them, demanding that Claude fill the gaps when I wasn't 100% clear. When it didn't understand me, I'd get indignant: "Well, obviously if I said this, then it should know I meant that." But no. That's not how it works. I've had to constantly remind myself that Claude is a machine. It works on algorithms and mechanics I don't all yet understand, but it's not actually thinking. It follows directions at all times.

I had to learn to adjust myself too. The communication contract goes both ways. I had to learn to be patient, and to explain myself fully so I could get what I want. The flaws I spent this whole essay pointing at? Half of them were really just me not being clear yet.

So the truth of it is simple. The end product is only as good as I am… as good as my ability to direct it. That's the skill. Not "using AI." Directing it. And that part is me.
